Bringing a Patient Home From Hospital: A Discharge and Home Care Checklist

SM
Author Srinika Medisurge Medical Team (Guided by Dr. Piyush Saxena) Srinika Medisurge Doorstep Healthcare, Gomti Nagar, Lucknow Medically reviewed by Dr. Piyush Saxena

Discharge day brings relief, but also worry. The patient is going home, yet the hospital team that watched them day and night will no longer be there. Many problems after discharge, like missed medicines, infected wounds or falls, can be avoided with a little planning. This checklist, put together by our medical team, helps families in Lucknow prepare.

Before Leaving the Hospital

Ask the ward doctor or nurse these questions and write down the answers:

  • Discharge summary: collect the full discharge summary along with all test reports, scans and the operation notes if there was surgery.
  • Medicine list: the name, dose, timing and duration of each medicine. Ask which old medicines should be stopped.
  • Wound, drain or catheter care: how often the dressing should be changed, when stitches will be removed, and when a catheter or drain should come out.
  • Diet and fluids: any restrictions on salt, sugar, water or solid food, and the feeding plan if the patient has a Ryle's tube.
  • Activity: how much walking is allowed, whether physiotherapy is needed and any movements to avoid.
  • Follow-up: the date of the next check-up and which tests are needed before it.
  • Warning signs: which symptoms mean you should call the doctor or come back to the hospital.

Setting Up the Room at Home

  • Choose a ground-floor room close to a bathroom if possible.
  • Clear the path from bed to bathroom; remove loose rugs and wires to prevent falls.
  • Keep a night light, a bell or phone within the patient's reach.
  • Arrange equipment before the patient arrives: hospital bed, air mattress, commode chair, wheelchair, oxygen concentrator or BiPAP if prescribed. See our medical equipment on rent.
  • Use a pill box marked by day and time, and keep a simple chart to tick each dose.

The First Week at Home

Keep a simple daily log

Note temperature, BP, pulse, oxygen level (if you have an oximeter), urine output, bowel movements, food intake and pain. This record helps the doctor make quick, correct decisions.

Don't skip dressings and procedures

Wounds need sterile dressing on schedule, and catheters, drains and Ryle's tubes need correct care. A nurse visit for wound dressing, catheter care or IV antibiotics saves the patient a tiring trip back to the hospital.

Start moving as advised

Lying in bed for too long weakens muscles and raises the risk of clots, chest infection and bedsores. Follow the activity plan, and start physiotherapy at home if the doctor recommends it. For patients who must stay in bed, read our bedsore prevention guide.

Plan who will do what

Decide who gives the medicines, who helps with bathing and toilet, and who stays at night. If the family can't manage round-the-clock care, a nurse, GDA or attendant can help. Our guide on GDA vs nurse vs attendant explains the difference.

Call the Doctor Promptly If You Notice

  • Fever, or redness, swelling, pus or bad smell from the wound.
  • New or increasing pain, swelling or pain in one calf.
  • Breathlessness, chest pain or falling oxygen levels.
  • Confusion, unusual drowsiness or a new weakness of the face, arm or leg.
  • Very little urine, blood in urine, or a blocked catheter.
  • Vomiting, inability to eat or drink, or no bowel movement for several days.

For a quick check without travelling, book a doctor home visit or an online consultation and keep your discharge summary ready to share.

Important Medical Disclaimer

This guide is for general education and does not replace a doctor's examination. If the patient has severe breathlessness, chest pain, heavy bleeding, fainting, confusion or a seizure, call the national emergency helpline 108 or go to the nearest hospital emergency department immediately.
